# Exemplar Logo Bank — 2026 reference set

**Purpose:** opinionated, curated set of 28 best-in-class marks. Used by `design-logo` Phase 1 (inspiration) and Phase 5/6 (benchmark target). Read BEFORE generating concepts. Re-read BEFORE self-scoring.

**How to use:**
1. Phase 1 — pick ≥3 entries whose *transferable principle* maps to your brief. Note them in `brand-brief.yaml` under `exemplar_inspiration:` (entry id + which principle).
2. Phase 5/6 — for your top 3 concepts, place each next to its claimed exemplar in a 2-up. If the exemplar visibly outclasses your concept on the same principle, your concept is not ≥65/70. Keep iterating or escalate to designer-handoff.
3. **Never copy the visual.** Copy the *principle* only. Each entry has a `do not copy` shadow.

**Provenance:** entries selected from publicly known brand identity work, 2018–2025 active marks. Year refers to current version, not founding. Sources are widely-discussed in brand-design press (Brand New / UnderConsideration, It's Nice That, The Brand Identity, Brandingmag). This file is a curated opinion, not exhaustive — refresh annually.

**Last refreshed:** 2026-05-01

## A. Wordmarks (logotype-only)

### A1 — Stripe (2010, refined ~2016)
- **Type:** custom geometric wordmark, lowercase, single-stroke "tr" ligature
- **Why exceptional:** does the boring well. Letterforms are mathematically tuned (counters, x-height, terminal weight) to read as engineered, not designed. Calm authority signal — banking trust without bank visual cliché.
- **Transferable principle:** for fintech / infra / B2B tools, custom-tuned grotesque wordmark > monogram + container. Restraint IS the brand.
- **Do not copy:** the lowercase + single accent color combo. Now a saturated SaaS template.

### A2 — Linear (2019)
- **Type:** custom geometric wordmark, optical-corrected lowercase, often paired with a triangular "L" glyph
- **Why exceptional:** every curve is optical-corrected, not mathematical. The "n" arches are subtly asymmetric to feel alive at small sizes. Pairs with a glyph that's a literal geometric primitive — confidence in plain-ness.
- **Transferable principle:** "primitive shape glyph + tuned wordmark" is a defensible pattern for dev tools. The glyph need not be ornate if the wordmark earns the brand.
- **Do not copy:** the literal triangle. It became a cliché within 2 years (Vercel ∆, Loops, every YC dev tool).

### A3 — Mubi (2016 rebrand)
- **Type:** all-caps custom wordmark, ultra-condensed sans, blue + white
- **Why exceptional:** rejects the "soft + friendly" streaming default. Editorial confidence — feels like a printed magazine masthead, not an app icon. Sets product positioning (curated arthouse) before user reads a word.
- **Transferable principle:** typography choice is brand positioning. Condensed editorial type signals "we curate, we don't algorithm."
- **Do not copy:** the literal condensed-blue treatment. Copy the discipline of letting type carry the entire brand.

### A4 — Aesop (1987, refined ongoing)
- **Type:** Optima-style serif wordmark, all-caps, no symbol
- **Why exceptional:** four decades, zero rebrand. Brand authority comes from refusing to chase trends. Wordmark is unmistakably theirs because everyone else moved to sans and they didn't.
- **Transferable principle:** stability is a brand asset. If your category is trend-chasing, classical type is differentiation.
- **Do not copy:** the literal Optima. Find your equivalent — what classical typography is your category ignoring?

### A5 — Pinterest 2024
- **Type:** custom script-influenced wordmark, lowercase, slight italic
- **Why exceptional:** the script "P" connects letters with subtle ligatures, signaling craft + handmade — directly mapping to product (visual inspiration / craft discovery). Brand & product intent aligned in the letterforms themselves.
- **Transferable principle:** letterform choice can encode product behavior. Ask: what does my product DO? Can the letterforms enact it?
- **Do not copy:** the script trend. By 2026 it's a saturated signal.

### A6 — Cash App "$" (2018+)
- **Type:** single-glyph mark — the dollar sign
- **Why exceptional:** owns a Unicode character. Every receipt, every keyboard, free brand reinforcement. Confidence to claim a primitive symbol that "shouldn't" be ownable.
- **Transferable principle:** can you own a primitive — a digit, a punctuation mark, a math symbol? Cheaper than custom geometry; instantly recognizable.
- **Do not copy:** literal currency symbols (saturated). Find an unowned glyph.

## B. Monograms / lettermarks

### B1 — Airbnb Bélo (2014, DesignStudio)
- **Type:** custom monogram — abstract A reading as person/place/heart/location-pin simultaneously
- **Why exceptional:** four readings in one glyph, all on-brand. Single mark resolves the brand's ambiguity (people OR places OR hospitality OR location) instead of forcing one. Mocked at launch ("genitalia"), now untouchable.
- **Transferable principle:** the strongest monogram resolves a brand's strategic tension into one glyph with multiple legitimate readings.
- **Do not copy:** the rounded curvilinear shape — it spawned a thousand SaaS marks. Copy the multi-reading discipline.

### B2 — Slack hash (2019 refresh, by Pentagram)
- **Type:** rotated octothorpe (#) made of 4 lozenges + 4 dots, 4 colors
- **Why exceptional:** rebrand from a chaotic flag shape into a single disciplined hash. Symbolizes channels (literal #) without being literal. Reduced color palette from 11 to 4 — discipline restored after years of inconsistency.
- **Transferable principle:** sometimes the brand symbol is the literal thing the product is named after. Don't be afraid of the obvious if it's executed with discipline.
- **Do not copy:** lozenge construction — became a 2020-era trope.

### B3 — Notion N (2016, refined 2020)
- **Type:** geometric serif monogram in black square
- **Why exceptional:** the only square + letter monogram on this list because it's the ONE that earned it. Serif + black + square reads "document," "page," "tool" all at once. Restraint where every competitor went rounded-square-color-gradient.
- **Transferable principle:** when category goes one way (rounded color), going the other way (sharp + black + serif) is differentiation.
- **Do not copy:** rounded square + letter + dot template. This is the saturated default — ironically what Notion *avoided*. (Per `framework-learnings.jsonl` confidence-9 entry.)

### B4 — Beats "b" (2008, Pentagram)
- **Type:** lowercase b inside a circle reading as a head wearing headphones
- **Why exceptional:** product depiction hidden in monogram. Glyph IS the product without being literal. Works on a ¢25 sticker and a $300 headphone shell.
- **Transferable principle:** if your product has a recognizable silhouette, can the monogram encode it as negative space?
- **Do not copy:** the circle container.

### B5 — Spotify (2015 refresh)
- **Type:** abstract glyph — three curved sound waves emanating from corner of a circle
- **Why exceptional:** wordmark abandoned for app-icon dominance. Glyph reads sound/audio/transmission instantly across cultures. One of the few brands where the symbol fully replaced the wordmark.
- **Transferable principle:** mobile-first products earn the right to drop the wordmark from the primary mark. Most B2B brands do not earn this — they think they do.
- **Do not copy:** the wave motif (saturated audio cliché now).

## C. Negative-space / hidden-meaning

### C1 — FedEx arrow (1994, Lindon Leader)
- **Type:** Wordmark with arrow hidden in negative space between E and x
- **Why exceptional:** the gold standard of negative space. 99% of viewers don't consciously see it; once seen, never unseen. Encodes brand promise (forward motion, delivery) in the typography itself, no extra glyph needed.
- **Transferable principle:** can the negative space between two letters of your wordmark encode meaning? It's a one-time payoff that lasts decades.
- **Do not copy:** the arrow. Any new arrow-in-negative-space reads as FedEx homage.

### C2 — Toblerone bear (1908, hidden in mountain)
- **Type:** Mark with bear silhouette hidden inside Matterhorn mountain
- **Why exceptional:** century-old mark with a hidden joke that rewards inspection. Brand identity = mountain + chocolate + city of origin (Bern = bear). Three brand facts in one negative space.
- **Transferable principle:** geographic / heritage clues can hide in a primary illustration. Check your brand's etymology — is there a literal symbol you're ignoring?
- **Do not copy:** illustrated landscape marks generally — they age poorly outside heritage brands.

### C3 — Tour de France (2003, by Joël Guenoun)
- **Type:** wordmark where lowercase "o" + "r" + "u" + "f" form a cyclist silhouette in negative space
- **Why exceptional:** wordmark IS the action. The discipline to hide the cyclist where most logos would draw a literal bicycle.
- **Transferable principle:** can the negative space between letters in your wordmark form the silhouette of the action your product enables?
- **Do not copy:** sports silhouette tropes.

## D. Iconic abstract marks

### D1 — NASA worm (1975, retired 1992, returned 2020)
- **Type:** custom geometric wordmark, all-caps, no crossbar on A's
- **Why exceptional:** the missing crossbars are the entire brand. A single geometric decision — remove the structural element of two letters — defines the mark for 50 years. Discipline of subtraction.
- **Transferable principle:** what can you *remove* from a standard letterform that becomes your signature? Subtraction is differentiation.
- **Do not copy:** the literal worm — sacred. Copy the subtraction discipline.

### D2 — Nike swoosh (1971, $35 by Carolyn Davidson)
- **Type:** abstract checkmark / wing swoosh
- **Why exceptional:** the proof that an abstract gesture can become a culturally dominant symbol if the brand pours decades of meaning into it. Mark itself is almost arbitrary — execution + consistency is everything.
- **Transferable principle:** don't over-engineer the mark. A clean abstract gesture + 30 years of consistency beats a clever mark + 3 rebrands.
- **Do not copy:** swoosh-shape gestures (saturated by athletics + B2C).

### D3 — Apple bitten apple (1977, refined 1998 monochrome)
- **Type:** mark — apple silhouette with bite, single color
- **Why exceptional:** the bite gives scale (bite = mouth size, so apple ≈ apple-sized) AND distinguishes from cherry/tomato silhouettes. One detail does two jobs.
- **Transferable principle:** any single deliberate detail in a mark should do two structural jobs (semantic + technical). If it only does one, simplify.
- **Do not copy:** fruit silhouettes. Sacred mark.

## E. Custom letterform / typographic identity

### E1 — Rivian R (2018)
- **Type:** geometric R lockup with horizontal bar above (oval/road element)
- **Why exceptional:** automotive sector defaults to shields/circles/wings. Rivian rejected all three for a sans-serif R + a horizontal element. Reads as adventure brand, not legacy auto.
- **Transferable principle:** when your category defaults to ornate symbolism (shields, crests, wings), going literal+geometric is positioning.
- **Do not copy:** the horizontal-bar treatment.

### E2 — Burberry 2023 (Daniel Lee, return to serif)
- **Type:** condensed serif wordmark + restored equestrian knight
- **Why exceptional:** *reverted* a 2018 sans-serif rebrand back to heritage serif + symbol. Public proof that homogenized luxury rebrands were a mistake. Heritage = differentiator.
- **Transferable principle:** for heritage brands, modernization-via-sans-serif erases the asset you're paid for. Reversal is sometimes the right rebrand.
- **Do not copy:** the literal knight. Copy the heritage-as-asset thinking.

### E3 — Pepsi 2023 (return to globe + 70s wordmark)
- **Type:** classic globe mark + bold condensed wordmark, tall "i"
- **Why exceptional:** acknowledged the 2008 rebrand was a mistake (and infamously expensive). Recovered visual equity by going back to recognizable 70s/90s elements.
- **Transferable principle:** don't underestimate visual equity. A 30-year-old mark customers already know is more valuable than a clever new one.
- **Do not copy:** literal globes.

### E4 — Eurostar 2023 (DesignStudio)
- **Type:** custom wordmark + abstract motion arc symbol
- **Why exceptional:** the symbol literally represents both train track curve AND the channel tunnel arc. One shape, two readings, both brand-specific. Wordmark is custom-cut to feel like infrastructure (calm, engineered, European).
- **Transferable principle:** if the brand has two products / two regions / two metaphors, can one curve encode both?
- **Do not copy:** abstract arc shapes (saturated travel cliché).

### E5 — Walmart 2025 refresh
- **Type:** wordmark refinement + retained spark, custom letter spacing
- **Why exceptional:** quiet refresh — same spark, same blue, custom-spaced letters. Restraint when most retailers would have done a full rebrand. Updates the mark without losing 60 years of recognition.
- **Transferable principle:** "refresh, don't rebrand" is often the right answer for established brands. Tighten what works; don't replace it.
- **Do not copy:** the spark itself; copy the quiet-refresh philosophy.

## F. Tech / 2024–2025 rebrand class

### F1 — Anthropic Claude (2024)
- **Type:** wordmark + abstract bloom / asterisk-petal symbol
- **Why exceptional:** rejected the "AI = blue gradient + abstract circle" cliché. Warm orange palette + organic bloom shape signals "approachable" without being childish. Differentiated from OpenAI/Google/Meta visual register.
- **Transferable principle:** when a category has a visual default (blue gradient, abstract circle), counter-positioning on color and shape is fast differentiation.
- **Do not copy:** organic bloom shapes (saturated post-2024).

### F2 — Vercel ∆ (2018, refined ongoing)
- **Type:** triangle (delta) glyph + wordmark
- **Why exceptional:** owns a math symbol (delta = change/diff). Glyph maps to product (deploys = deltas). Pure geometric primitive — works at 16 px favicon, 256 px hero, billboards.
- **Transferable principle:** geometric primitive + semantic meaning = scalable across all sizes and contexts. Hard to age.
- **Do not copy:** the triangle (now a saturated dev-tool default; see Linear note).

### F3 — Arc browser (2022, The Browser Company)
- **Type:** custom wordmark with arched "A" — type encodes the product name
- **Why exceptional:** the A literally arcs. Letterform = product name = brand promise (alternative arc through web browsing). One decision encodes everything.
- **Transferable principle:** can a single letter in your wordmark literally illustrate the product name's meaning?
- **Do not copy:** arched A's (will saturate).

### F4 — Raycast (2020, refined 2023)
- **Type:** custom geometric wordmark + red glyph
- **Why exceptional:** for a power-user tool, the brand reads as product confidence not product friendliness. Sharp angles + saturated red signal "for serious users." Rejects the soft pastel SaaS default.
- **Transferable principle:** brand can self-select your audience. Sharp + saturated = power user. Soft + pastel = mass consumer. Pick on purpose.
- **Do not copy:** specific letterforms.

## G. Instructive failure / cautionary case

### G1 — Jaguar 2024 rebrand (cautionary)
- **Type:** custom wordmark with mixed-case letterforms + new monogram
- **Why exceptional (as warning):** removed the leaper, removed the heritage shield, replaced with a fashion-house wordmark that fit no obvious automotive category. Public reception was hostile. Useful as the *anti-pattern* for heritage rebrands.
- **Transferable principle:** if you erase 90 years of brand equity in one rebrand, the new mark must do extraordinary work justifying the loss. Most don't.
- **Do not copy:** anything literal. Reference it when the user wants to "modernize away from heritage" — show this case before agreeing.

### G2 — Twitter → X (2023)
- **Type:** geometric X glyph
- **Why exceptional (as warning):** abandoned one of the most valuable brand assets in software (the bird, "tweet" as verb). Replaced with a generic X that competed with dozens of other X-named products. Useful proof that brand asset destruction is a real cost.
- **Transferable principle:** named verbs ("to tweet," "to google," "to slack") are uncopyable brand assets. Don't replace them with generic glyphs unless the strategic shift demands it.
- **Do not copy:** generic letter glyphs.

## H. Hospitality / service / local-business adjacent

### H1 — Airbnb Bélo (see B1 — also fits this category)

### H2 — Le Pain Quotidien (custom wordmark, decades-old)
- **Type:** custom serif wordmark, calligraphic
- **Why exceptional:** for a bakery/café chain, type quality + ink-on-paper quality replace the need for any symbol. Reads as artisan-craft because the type IS artisan-craft.
- **Transferable principle:** for hospitality brands serving food/drink/service, custom typography often outclasses an icon. The type is the brand.
- **Do not copy:** generic script fonts (the cheap version of this principle).

### H3 — Toast POS (restaurant tech, 2018+)
- **Type:** wordmark + warm orange palette
- **Why exceptional (as benchmark for Example Marketplace-class brief):** competing in restaurant SaaS, chose warm hospitality-coded palette + simple wordmark over tech-coded blue/teal. Brand reads as "made by people who've worked a Friday night service," not "made by enterprise software vendors."
- **Transferable principle:** for vertical SaaS (restaurants, salons, retail), color palette should code the *served industry*, not the *software industry*. Warm > cool. Hospitality > infra.
- **Do not copy:** the literal palette. Copy the cross-coding insight.

## I. Vibecode / AI / breakout product marks 2024–2025

Real PNG/SVG assets for these are downloaded to `exemplar-bank-images/originals/` (see manifest there). Always view the actual file when benchmarking — descriptions are scaffolding, not the comparison target.

### I1 — Cursor (2023→, AI code editor)
- **Type:** wordmark + custom geometric glyph (cursor/caret abstraction)
- **Why exceptional:** primitive shape (cursor) → semantic mapping (code editor). Resists the "AI = bloom/orb/circle" cliché. Mark works at favicon size on tabs full of dev tools.
- **Transferable principle:** for tools that operate on a primitive (cursor, file, line, prompt), the primitive IS the mark.
- **Do not copy:** caret/cursor glyphs (now saturated post-Cursor success).

### I2 — Bolt.new (2024, StackBlitz)
- **Type:** wordmark with bolt glyph
- **Why exceptional:** brand promise (instant / electric / fast) literally encoded in mark. Single-syllable name + literal symbol = unmistakable in ad creative.
- **Transferable principle:** if your brand name IS a noun, the mark can be the noun. Stop searching for abstraction.
- **Do not copy:** lightning-bolt motif (saturated by every "fast" SaaS).

### I3 — Lovable (2024)
- **Type:** wordmark with heart accent
- **Why exceptional:** consumer-grade warmth in a developer tool category dominated by cold geometry. Brand counter-positioning via emotional palette.
- **Transferable principle:** if your category defaults cold/serious, going warm/playful is positioning leverage. (Same insight as Toast POS H3.)
- **Do not copy:** literal hearts.

### I4 — v0 (2023, Vercel)
- **Type:** ultra-minimal wordmark — letter + digit
- **Why exceptional:** owns a version-number-as-name. Mark is just "v0" set in a refined sans. Brand is restraint.
- **Transferable principle:** if you can name yourself a primitive (number, symbol, math notation), the wordmark needs almost nothing else.
- **Do not copy:** version-number naming (one-shot trick).

### I5 — Replit (2024 refresh)
- **Type:** custom geometric glyph + wordmark, refreshed away from older "rocket" mark
- **Why exceptional:** rebrand to drop literal/illustrative element in favor of disciplined geometric symbol. Aged-up the brand without losing recognition.
- **Transferable principle:** rebranding from literal/illustrative → geometric/symbolic is the standard "we grew up" move. Do it once; don't oscillate.
- **Do not copy:** specific glyph.

### I6 — Windsurf (2024, formerly Codeium)
- **Type:** custom geometric glyph + wordmark
- **Why exceptional:** category renamed itself. Brand picked an unusual product metaphor (windsurfing = balance + flow + solo skill) and committed. Differentiates from Cursor's terminal-coded register.
- **Transferable principle:** unusual product metaphors create unowned visual territory. The cost is teaching the metaphor; the payoff is uncopyable.
- **Do not copy:** sport metaphors (saturating).

### I7 — Granola (2024, AI meeting notes)
- **Type:** wordmark + soft warm-coded glyph
- **Why exceptional:** B2B productivity tool with consumer-snack name + warm earth palette. Rejects the entire "enterprise AI" visual register. Stands out in a screen full of Slack/Notion icons.
- **Transferable principle:** consumer-coded brand language for B2B tools is differentiation when your buyer is the end user (not procurement).
- **Do not copy:** literal food motifs.

### I8 — Perplexity (2023, refined 2024)
- **Type:** wordmark + custom geometric glyph (rounded square + abstract symbol)
- **Why exceptional:** survived as a real consumer brand among LLM products. Mark works in the URL bar, in mobile app store, in TV ads — the rare "scales across all surfaces" AI mark.
- **Transferable principle:** scalability across all surfaces (favicon → app icon → billboard) is a brand asset most AI brands fail. Test early.
- **Do not copy:** rounded-square+letter (saturated; see B3 Notion warning).

### I9 — ElevenLabs (2023→)
- **Type:** wordmark + abstract sound-wave glyph
- **Why exceptional:** clean editorial type for an AI voice/audio brand. Mark feels like a record label, not a software tool — appropriate for product positioning (premium voice production).
- **Transferable principle:** if your product replaces a creative-industry tool, dress like that industry, not like SaaS.
- **Do not copy:** sound-wave motif (saturated audio cliché).

### I10 — Suno (2024, AI music)
- **Type:** wordmark + simple geometric glyph
- **Why exceptional:** approachable consumer brand for a "scary AI replaces musicians" product. Disarms the threat through visual register choice.
- **Transferable principle:** controversial-product brands need approachable marks. Visual hostility compounds the product's threat.
- **Do not copy:** specific glyph.

### I11 — Krea (2024, AI image)
- **Type:** wordmark, custom geometric
- **Why exceptional:** disciplined wordmark in a category (AI image-gen) full of psychedelic/maximalist marks. Counter-positioning works again.
- **Transferable principle:** when your category goes maximalist, minimalism is the leverage move.

### I12 — Runway (2018→, AI creative tools)
- **Type:** wordmark + brand system (often paired with vibrant gradients for marketing, but the mark itself is restrained)
- **Why exceptional:** restraint at the mark level + maximalism at the marketing-system level. Two registers, one brand. Lets product feel premium AND playful.
- **Transferable principle:** the mark and the marketing system can play different registers. Mark = forever; system = seasonal.

### I13 — Wispr Flow (2024)
- **Type:** wordmark + small accent glyph
- **Why exceptional:** soft type + breathing-related metaphor for a voice-input product. Brand language enacts product behavior (whisper → voice).
- **Transferable principle:** type weight and softness are part of brand voice. Pick them on purpose.

### I14 — OpenAI (2022→)
- **Type:** wordmark + flower/petal abstract glyph
- **Why exceptional:** the original "AI = abstract organic shape" mark — still valid because it shipped first. Sets the template every other AI brand now reacts against.
- **Transferable principle:** being first to a visual register is its own moat. Reacting to incumbents requires explicit counter-positioning.
- **Do not copy:** organic petal/bloom shapes (saturated by Anthropic, Mistral, Cohere, etc.).

### I15 — Mistral (2024)
- **Type:** wordmark + abstract geometric glyph
- **Why exceptional:** European AI lab brand register — calmer, more editorial than US AI brands. Geographic/cultural identity is part of the differentiation.
- **Transferable principle:** geographic/cultural register can differentiate even within a saturated category.

### I16 — Groq (2024)
- **Type:** wordmark, geometric custom-cut, often paired with orange palette
- **Why exceptional:** infra brand that looks like infra — sharp angles, no warmth, no softness. Brand confidence to feel cold for a cold-but-fast product.
- **Transferable principle:** match brand temperature to product reality. Don't warm up cold products.

### I17 — Cognition (Devin, 2024)
- **Type:** wordmark + abstract glyph
- **Why exceptional:** academic-coded brand register (Cognition) for a controversial product (autonomous engineer). Brand softens product threat through register choice.

### I18 — Pika (2024, AI video)
- **Type:** wordmark with playful custom letterforms
- **Why exceptional:** consumer-creator brand register — closer to TikTok than to Adobe. Picked a side and committed.
- **Transferable principle:** for creator-economy tools, prosumer-coded brand > pro-coded brand. The audience self-selects via the mark.

### I19 — Midjourney (2022→)
- **Type:** wordmark + abstract sailing-ship glyph
- **Why exceptional:** owns an unusual metaphor (mid-journey, voyage). Geometric ship glyph is unmistakable in a sea of abstract circles.
- **Transferable principle:** unusual literal metaphors (when committed to) outperform safe abstractions.
- **Do not copy:** literal vessels.

### I20 — Captions (2024)
- **Type:** wordmark + simple symbol
- **Why exceptional:** straightforward consumer creator brand. Functional naming + functional mark — confidence to NOT be clever.
- **Transferable principle:** functional clarity > clever obscurity for tools where users find you via search/recommendation.

### J1 — FedEx (1994)
- **Type:** wordmark
- **Why exceptional:** The negative-space arrow between E and x is discovered, not announced. It rewards a second look without demanding it.
- **Transferable principle:** hidden meaning must be DISCOVERABLE — visible when pointed out, not invisible forever. The best hooks sit in the gap between "obvious" and "hidden."
- **Hook discoverability:** high — the arrow is geometrically inevitable given the letterforms; it doesn't require a stretch.

### J2 — Toblerone (1970)
- **Type:** wordmark + mountain pictogram
- **Why exceptional:** A bear is hidden in the negative space of the Matterhorn. It connects product origin (Bern, Switzerland, city of bears) to brand mark without literal illustration.
- **Transferable principle:** hidden meaning should carry brand DNA — not random Easter eggs, but origin-story or value-story compression.
- **Hook discoverability:** medium-low — most viewers never see the bear until told; this is acceptable because the mountain itself is strong enough standalone.

### J3 — Amazon (2000)
- **Type:** wordmark
- **Why exceptional:** The a-to-z smile curve encodes range (everything from a to z) and friendliness in one stroke. It's a functional promise, not decoration.
- **Transferable principle:** hidden meaning can be FUNCTIONAL — it explains what the brand does, not just where it comes from.
- **Hook discoverability:** high — the smile is visible immediately; the a-to-z reading comes second.

### J4 — Hershey's Kisses (1970s)
- **Type:** wordmark
- **Why exceptional:** A Kiss shape is hidden in the negative space between the K and the i, and between the s and the final e. The product IS the hidden letter.
- **Transferable principle:** the hidden shape can be the product itself — when the product has a simple silhouette.
- **Hook discoverability:** medium — requires clean typography and enough spacing for the shape to breathe.

### J5 — Tour de France (2002)
- **Type:** wordmark
- **Why exceptional:** The letter R doubles as a cyclist, and the yellow dot (the sun / the maillot jaune) completes the scene. The mark IS the event.
- **Transferable principle:** hidden meaning can be narrative — it tells a micro-story about what the brand does.
- **Hook discoverability:** high — the cyclist is visible at second glance because the R's leg is naturally shaped.

### J6 — Pittsburgh Zoo (2009)
- **Type:** pictogram
- **Why exceptional:** A gorilla and a lion face each other in negative space inside a tree. Two animals, one shape — structural cleverness.
- **Transferable principle:** figure-ground reversal can hide TWO meanings at once, not just one.
- **Hook discoverability:** medium — the tree is dominant; animals emerge on closer inspection.

### J7 — Wendy's (1969, updated 2013)
- **Type:** wordmark + mascot
- **Why exceptional:** The word "mom" is hidden in the collar of the mascot. It's a warmth signal embedded in the character design.
- **Transferable principle:** hidden meaning can be emotional — it encodes a brand value (family, warmth, trust) rather than a functional claim.
- **Hook discoverability:** low — most viewers never see it; the collar is decorative until pointed out. This is acceptable for emotional hooks because the primary mark must stand alone.

### J8 — Baskin-Robbins (2005)
- **Type:** wordmark
- **Why exceptional:** The number 31 (31 flavors) is hidden in the pink parts of the B and R. A numerical brand promise embedded in the initials.
- **Transferable principle:** hidden meaning can be a NUMBER or quantified promise — useful for brands with a specific claim.
- **Hook discoverability:** medium — the pink highlights draw the eye; the 31 emerges on second look.

### J9 — Tostitos (2003)
- **Type:** wordmark
- **Why exceptional:** The two t's in the center are people sharing a chip over a bowl of salsa (the dot on the i). The brand IS the social moment.
- **Transferable principle:** hidden meaning can be a SCENE — it depicts the brand's core experience, not just its product.
- **Hook discoverability:** high — the people are visible once you look at the t's as figures; the salsa dot completes the story.

### J10 — Galeries Lafayette (2019)
- **Type:** wordmark
- **Why exceptional:** The G and L ligature forms a single flowing gesture that suggests both luxury and accessibility. No hidden object, but the LETTERFORM itself carries dual meaning through its construction.
- **Transferable principle:** hidden meaning can live in LETTERFORM CONSTRUCTION — the way letters are drawn can encode two registers (heritage + modern, luxury + playful) without adding extra shapes.
- **Hook discoverability:** high — the ligature is visible immediately; the dual-register reading comes with context.

## How to compose your inspiration set (Phase 1)

For any new brief, pick ≥3 entries from this bank where the *transferable principle* maps to the brief — not where the visual matches. Record in `brand-brief.yaml`:

```yaml
exemplar_inspiration:
  - id: A2  # Linear
    principle: "primitive shape glyph + optical-corrected wordmark"
    why_relevant: "<brief reason for this brand>"
  - id: H3  # Toast POS
    principle: "warm hospitality palette over cool tech palette"
    why_relevant: "<brief reason>"
  - id: C1  # FedEx
    principle: "negative-space arrow as brand promise"
    why_relevant: "<brief reason — only if literal alignment exists>"
```

If you can't pick 3 with genuine principle alignment, the brief is too vague — push back to Phase 0 before generating concepts.

## Refresh policy

- Re-read this file at the start of EVERY logo run, even if you ran logo work last week.

## 1. Amazon WBR (Weekly Business Review)

**STABLE** — Framework mechanics don't drift; attendance/timing may vary.

### Mechanics
Amazon runs WBR every Wednesday morning (60 min standard; 90 min during peak/holiday). The prior week's metrics are generated by Sunday midnight. Tuesday: departmental WBRs cascade company-wide. Wednesday: company-level WBR with tight choreography.

Three explicit questions it must answer in order:
1. What did customers experience last week?
2. How did our business perform last week?
3. Are we on track to hit targets?

### The critical distinction: Input vs Output
The WBR is built around **controllable input metrics** — actions directly proven to move output metrics. Executives are NOT permitted to discuss output metrics (revenue, DAU/MAU, contribution margin) in a problem-solving sense; only in a reporting sense. The reason: output metrics are retrospective, inputs are actionable.

Example: "Add 500 new products to Musical Instruments" is an input metric. "Musical Instruments revenue grew 12%" is an output metric. You manage inputs; you report outputs.

### Exception-driven format
If a metric shows routine variation, the owner states "nothing to see here" and the meeting moves on within seconds. Only exceptional variation triggers discussion. Metric owners must either explain the cause or say "I don't know and am still investigating." No speculation permitted. Finance independently audits all metrics — no skin in the game.

### Visualization standards (three types only)
1. **6-12 Graphs**: Six-week zoomed view + trailing 12-month monthly view on same X-axis. Numbers printed directly on graph.
2. **6-12 Tables**: Multiple metrics together when lines would clutter a graph.
3. **Plain Tables**: Context data (competitor pricing, latency comparisons).

### Common failure modes
Bloated attendance lists, growing metric count, accusatory tone, clock-running on items where no one will own a resolution. All four are documented failure patterns in Bryar & Carr's analysis (source: commoncog.com).

### Why the band: WBR input metric targets set at ~75% expected full achievement
Amazon deliberately sets targets at the top of what is realistically attainable. Only ~75% are expected to be fully hit. Consistently hitting 100% signals targets are too easy. This band is STABLE because it reflects a cultural calibration, not a market condition.

## 2. Amazon 6-Pager

**STABLE** — Format established, documented in "Working Backwards" (Bryar & Carr).

### Structure
Six sections, narrative prose (not bullet points except in Goals and Tenets):
1. **Introduction** — frame the problem/opportunity
2. **Goals** — can be bulleted; what success looks like
3. **Tenets** — principles guiding decisions in this space
4. **State of the Business** — current reality, data-backed
5. **Lessons Learned** — what has been tried, what was learned
6. **Strategic Priorities** — the ask, the direction, the specific next actions

Appendices are data-dense. The narrative body references appendix data but does not embed charts — readers consult appendix separately.

**Silent read time: 20-25 minutes at meeting start.** Everyone reads before discussion begins. No pre-briefing allowed (negates the silence benefit). Meeting attendees mark up their printed copies.

### Why narrative, not slides
Slides hide weak thinking behind bullet fragments. A narrative that compels full sentences forces the author to complete the reasoning. Amazon found this produced higher-quality proposals and exposed faulty assumptions faster. The reader must actively process prose — a bulleted deck allows passive absorption.

### WBR vs 6-pager relationship
WBR = weekly operational review (input metrics, exceptions, no strategy).
6-pager = decision memo for strategic or investment proposals (separate S-team meeting, not WBR).
They are distinct operating mechanisms that must NOT be conflated.

## 3. OKR Cadence

**VOLATILE rows: completion rates, adoption percentages** — these shift as practice matures.
**STABLE rows: scoring framework, cadence structure.**

### Why quarterly cycles
Quarterly cadence allows four strategy adjustments per year instead of one. Monthly OKRs exist but create planning overhead that exceeds the benefit for most teams. Annual OKRs lose relevance by Q3.

### Scoring: why 0.7-0.8 is the sweet spot
Google's 0.0–1.0 scale (original OKR popularizer) was designed so that 0.7-0.8 = "I nailed a hard goal." Consistently hitting 1.0 signals the goal was too easy — you sandbag to look good. The discomfort of 0.6 is productive; the comfort of 1.0 is a warning sign. The framework bands here are **STABLE** because they reflect the original Google design intent, which has held for 20+ years.

### Scoring method adoption (VOLATILE)
As of 2025-2026 surveys (okrstool.com):
- 55% use percentage completion (0–100%) — most common
- 16% use Google-style 0.0–1.0
- 13% use qualitative review only

These percentages will shift as OKR maturity spreads. Re-verify on each 30-day window refresh.

### Weekly check-in lift (+43%)
Teams that run a structured weekly check-in against their OKRs complete 43% more of them than teams without. The check-in does not need to be long — 15-30 min per team is sufficient. The discipline of named accountability is the mechanism, not the meeting length.

### First-cycle realism
First-time OKR adopters average ~51% completion. This is not failure — it reflects learning the cadence. Expect 5 cycles to reach top-quartile performance (~79%). Set executive expectations accordingly.

## 4. Bezos One-Way / Two-Way Door (Type 1 / Type 2 Decisions)

**STABLE** — Framework principle from Bezos 2015 shareholder letter; unchanged.

### The classification
**Type 2 (two-way door):** Reversible, low-cost to undo. Examples: new UI layout, a marketing campaign, adding a product feature. Make fast; delegate down; default to experimentation. Treat spending >2 days of exec time on a Type 2 as an escalation failure.

**Type 1 (one-way door):** Largely irreversible or very costly to undo. Examples: acquiring a company, shutting down a product line, changing pricing model company-wide. Require written narrative (often a 6-pager), explicit risk mapping, and slow deliberate process.

### The failure mode this framework prevents
Large-company slowness applied to small decisions. Startups default Type 1 protocols to everything — requiring committee consensus on a feature tweak — and lose speed advantage over incumbents. The framework forces classification before applying process.

### Practical classification test
Ask: "If we do this and it fails, can we reverse it in < 2 weeks for < $10K of cost?" If yes → Type 2, decide fast. If no → Type 1, write a memo.

### Why this is STABLE
The framework is a reasoning heuristic, not a market statistic. The cost/time thresholds for classification in YOUR context may differ — scale them to company size — but the underlying principle does not drift with market conditions.

## 5. RICE Prioritization

**STABLE** — Formula and impact scale are framework constants; relative ranking is definitionally non-benchmarkable.

### Formula
```
RICE Score = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) / Effort
```

**Reach:** How many people affected per quarter (absolute number, not percentage).
**Impact:** 3 = massive / 2 = high / 1 = medium / 0.5 = low / 0.25 = minimal (choose one).
**Confidence:** % certainty in estimates. <50% = moonshot. Start at 50% for unknowns. Raise only with real evidence.
**Effort:** Person-months to complete.

### Why there is no universal threshold
RICE is a relative ranking tool. A score of 500 means nothing absolute — it means "higher than everything scoring <500 in your backlog this quarter." Never gate on RICE score; gate on RICE rank.

### The confidence <50% rule
Below 50% confidence, the estimate is so uncertain that the RICE score is dominated by noise. Deprioritize — put in discovery backlog, not the roadmap. This rule is STABLE and design-original (Intercom, the framework's source).

### RICE vs RICE
RICE was not designed for strategic decisions — it was designed for feature prioritization within a product. Do not apply it to org-level or market-level decisions. Use Bezos Door Test for strategic classification and 6-pager for strategic proposals.

## 6. DRI (Directly Responsible Individual)

**STABLE** — Organizational pattern from Apple; principle is decades-old.

### Mechanics
One named person appears next to every action item, project, and outcome. Not a committee. Not a team. One human name. That person is solely accountable for the result.

DRI is visible in meeting agendas (their name appears next to the topic), in project trackers, and in review meetings. The DRI may delegate work but cannot delegate accountability.

### Why single ownership works
Decision-by-committee diffuses accountability and slows decisions. When everyone owns something, no one owns it. DRI forces clarity: one person can be promoted or corrected based on outcome.

### DRI vs Scrum ownership
Scrum assigns sprint tasks to team members, but product ownership is often shared or diffuse. DRI is broader — it applies to entire outcomes (launch a product, hit a retention target, close a partnership), not just sprint tasks.

### Startup application
In a 5-person startup, DRI is often implicit. The framework becomes critical at 15-30 people when parallel workstreams create ambiguity. Formalize DRI assignment before scaling, not after the first accountability failure.

## 7. Default-Alive Gate

**VOLATILE** — The runway thresholds and fundraising timelines shift with market conditions.

### The core test (Paul Graham, paulgraham.com/aord.html)
At current burn (expenses constant) and current revenue growth rate: does the company reach profitability before cash runs out?

Yes → **Default Alive.** Raising more capital is optionality, not necessity.
No → **Default Dead.** Survival depends on an external event (fundraise, revenue spike). This is not fatal — but it must be named explicitly, not obscured by optimistic projections.

### The calculation (Graham / Blackwell)
Use Trevor Blackwell's calculator at `growth.tlb.org`. Inputs: current MRR, monthly MRR growth rate, monthly burn, cash remaining. Output: months to profitability or cash-out.

### 2026 runway benchmarks (VOLATILE — re-research monthly)
- **Seed round size**: Median $3.0M (Q1 2026, US; source: peony.ink Q1 2026 benchmarks)
- **Seed runway at close**: Median 18 months; burn $75K–$100K/month
- **Minimum safe runway** (new 2026 floor): 24–30 months
  - Seed → Series A median time: **774 days (2.1 years)**, up from 420 days in 2021 — an 84% increase
  - The old 18-month planning rule is now **structurally insufficient**
- **Series A**: Median $10M–$15M, pre-money $40M–$55M

### Ramen profitability (VOLATILE)
~$5K–$10K/month combined founder living costs (Western context). Reaching ramen profitability = default alive by definition. Threshold scales with location/team; re-verify for non-Western contexts.

### The "5× YoY" investor-dependency exception (STABLE)
Graham's guidance: only companies growing >5× year-over-year can somewhat rely on investor interest as a Plan B. At lower growth rates, treat investor dependency as default dead, not a plan.

### Overhiring as the primary kill mechanism
Graham identifies overhiring as "by far the biggest killer of startups that raise money." DRI model + default-alive monitoring are the structural defenses: named owners expose headcount creep; default-alive gate reveals the burn impact in weeks, not quarters.

## 8. Contradiction-Mapping Synthesis

**STABLE** — Method principle; no market benchmarks apply.

### What it is
When a WBR or OKR review surfaces two apparently contradictory data points (e.g., NPS score up 12 points; churn rate also up 3%), contradiction mapping is the structured approach for processing both as simultaneously true before resolving either.

### The process
1. **State both as facts.** Do not explain one away. Write: "NPS is up 12. Churn is up 3%."
2. **Hypothesize the gap mechanism.** What could make both true? (Example: new cohort loves the product but churns because of onboarding failure, not product quality.)
3. **Name the mechanism as a testable hypothesis.** Assign a DRI. Set a 2-WBR deadline to validate or refute.
4. **Do not resolve in the WBR.** The operational meeting is not the right forum for root-cause debate. Surface and escalate.

### Why this matters for operating cadence
Premature resolution of contradictions ("the churn is seasonal, ignore it") hides causal structure. Amazon's WBR design explicitly forbids speculation for this reason — when two metrics conflict, the correct response is "I don't know and am investigating," not a narrative that makes the conflict disappear.

### Connection to WBR
Contradiction-mapping is an informal but widely practiced technique that emerges from the WBR's "no speculation" rule. It is not an Amazon-named framework — it is the practical output of disciplined operational reviews where both signals are respected.

## Weekly / Quarterly Cadence Design

**STABLE** — Meeting cadence principles don't drift; time allocations are reasonable defaults, not market benchmarks.

### Recommended startup operating rhythm

| Cadence | Format | Length | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Daily** | Stand-up | 15 min | Blockers only; no status |
| **Weekly** | Team sync | 30–45 min | Input metrics, OKR check-in, DRI callouts |
| **Weekly** | 1:1 (manager-IC) | 25–30 min | Coaching, blockers, career |
| **Monthly** | All-hands | 45–60 min | Company health, wins, risks |
| **Quarterly** | OKR planning | 90–120 min | Set next quarter OKRs; grade last quarter |
| **Quarterly** | Board/investor | 3 hours | Pre-read package; 30 min Q&A + 2.5h spotlight |

### Pre-reads and written artifacts
Quarterly planning: pre-reads 48 hours in advance. Board updates: each exec writes 1-2 page functional update (6-pager format if complex). Rule: **come with data, not opinions. Leave with written commitments.**

### The cadence audit rule
Review meeting cadence itself every quarter. Meeting structures calcify and lose relevance. The best cadence is the one that gets revisited.

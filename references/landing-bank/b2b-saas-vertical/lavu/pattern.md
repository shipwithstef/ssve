# Lavu — The iPad POS built for restaurants by restaurant people

**URL:** https://lavu.com
**Captured:** 2026-04-27 (pattern.md only; hero.png/.webm TODO-capture)
**Sector:** b2b-saas-vertical (iPad-first restaurant POS)

## What it does well

- **Hero hardware-narrative: "iPad-first, designed for the rail, not the back office."** Specifies the form-factor commitment as a value prop — iPad-only operators are an underserved segment Toast/Square serve indifferently. Owns the niche.
- **Hero subhead names cuisine types served.** "Pizza shops, coffee bars, food trucks, full-service" — buyer self-qualifies fast. Notably small-format operators (food trucks) are called out, which most competitors ignore.
- **Operator-quote hero overlay, not a generic testimonial section.** A single restaurant-owner quote is layered ON the hero photo: "Switched from Aloha in 3 days, kept all my menu data" — front-loads the migration objection.
- **"Lavu vs. Toast" comparison page is linked from hero nav.** Doesn't shy from naming the giant; positions on iPad-first + pricing transparency where Lavu wins.
- **Pricing page shows monthly + hardware bundle in one row.** Most POS pricing pages separate software and hardware costs, hiding the true monthly outlay. Lavu shows the all-in number, which builds trust even though it's higher.
- **Integrations page calls out the cuisine-specific ones first.** Pizza-toppings inventory, coffee-bar timing modifiers, food-truck mobile-payments — niche integrations that prove "we actually know your business."
- **Customer support promise is in-hero: "24/7 US-based support."** Restaurant-operators run nights/weekends; this is load-bearing. Visible in hero, not buried in footer.
- **Inventory-tracking demo with actual pizza-shop SKUs.** Demo data isn't generic ("Item 1, Item 2") — uses real menu items (pepperoni, mozzarella, dough) so pizza operators see themselves immediately.
- **Lavu Capital (financing) cross-sell sub-page.** Hardware-financing for cash-strapped operators — removes the upfront-cost objection that loses small-format buyers to "free" software with hidden hardware costs.

## What it gets wrong

- The "by restaurant people for restaurant people" tagline is overused on the page — appears 4 times in different forms. Becomes noise.
- Hero CTA is "Get a quote" — friction for self-serve iPad-only operators. "Start free trial" would convert better for the small-format buyers Lavu is targeting.
- Comparison vs. Toast sub-page underplays Toast's hardware advantages — feels selectively honest, which sophisticated buyers detect.

## Reusable archetype name

**Form-factor-commitment hero with niche cuisine callouts** — hero leads with a specific hardware/form-factor commitment that competitors treat as commodity, niche operator types are explicitly named to win underserved segments, all-in pricing transparency used as wedge against bundled-pricing-hiders.

## When to use this archetype

When the product makes a deliberate form-factor or platform choice that competitors treat as one-of-many AND there's a long tail of niche operator types your competitors ignore. NOT a fit for: platform-agnostic products, products serving only mainstream segments.

## Feature mix surfaced

- "iPad-first POS designed for the rail": hero headline + iPad-in-hand visual
- "Built for pizza shops, coffee bars, food trucks, full-service": hero subhead + cuisine-specific sub-pages
- "24/7 US-based support — we answer when your line is out the door": hero badge + support-team photo mid-page

## Adoption notes

When porting this archetype, the most common mistake is copying the surface aesthetic without copying the underlying buyer-context that makes it work. Verify the buyer-segment fit BEFORE imitating the visual signature — the archetype name names the technique, not just the look. If the buyer-context match is weak, downgrade to a borrowed sub-element (one bullet) rather than the whole pattern.
